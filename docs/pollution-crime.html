Pollution and Crime

Week 10 · Pigouvian taxes, the Coase theorem, and social multipliers

Read: Brueckner, Chapters 9 and 10; Currie et al. (2015); Chalfin and McCrary (2017).

The model

Cities put people close together, which makes both pollution and crime more costly. Both topics turn on externalities and on how people respond to incentives.

Pollution: Pigou and Coase

A factory that ignores the harm its emissions cause to neighbors produces where price equals its marginal private cost. The efficient output sets price equal to marginal social cost, private cost plus the marginal damage to others:

\[ P = MPC(Q) + MD(Q). \]

A Pigouvian tax equal to marginal damage at the efficient output makes the factory face the full cost. The Coase theorem offers another route: if property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, the factory and its neighbors can negotiate to the efficient outcome on their own. In a dense city with thousands of affected residents, bargaining is rarely cheap.

Crime: incentives and social interactions

In Gary Becker’s model, a person commits a crime when the benefit exceeds the expected cost: the probability of being caught, \(p\), times the punishment, \(f\). Raising either deters crime, but the evidence favors certainty over severity.

People also respond to one another. If each person’s behavior depends partly on what their peers do, a policy that directly reduces crime by some amount sets off a chain of further reductions. With individual crime rate \(a = b + s\,\bar{a}\), where \(b\) reflects a person’s own incentives, \(\bar{a}\) the average among peers, and \(0 < s < 1\) the strength of peer effects, the equilibrium is

\[ a = \frac{b}{1 - s}. \]

The factor \(1/(1-s)\) is the social multiplier.

Simulation 1: A Pigouvian tax

The factory sells at price \(P\) and has marginal private cost \(MPC = 20 + mQ\) and marginal damage \(MD = dQ\). Starting values match Problem B2 on Midterm 2.

viewof m = Inputs.range([0.5, 2], {value: 1, step: 0.05, label: "Private cost slope, m"})

QmaxP = Qp * 1.3

Things to try

  1. In Simulation 1, confirm the midterm answers: private output 60, efficient output 40, a $20 tax, and a $300 deadweight loss.
  2. Set marginal damage to zero. What happens to the tax and the deadweight loss? Why?
  3. Raise the damage slope. Does the efficient output fall by a lot or a little? What does that suggest about how stringent pollution rules should be near dense neighborhoods?
  4. In Simulation 2, set \(s = 0\). What is the multiplier? Now raise \(s\) to 0.8. Why do small differences in incentives produce big differences in crime when peer effects are strong?
  5. Peer effects amplify bad shocks too. Use Simulation 2 to explain how a small rise in unemployment could produce a large rise in crime.

What the evidence says

What people pay to avoid pollution. Currie et al. (2015) study about 1,600 openings and closings of industrial plants that emit toxic pollutants. Comparing homes very close to a plant with homes slightly farther away, before and after, they find that nearby home values fall when a plant opens and recover after it closes, and that infants born close to plants are in worse health. House prices reveal what people are willing to pay for cleaner air, the hedonic approach.

What deters crime. Chalfin and McCrary (2017) review the evidence and conclude that increases in the certainty of being caught, especially through more police, reduce crime more reliably than increases in the severity of punishment, such as longer sentences. Offenders may weigh the immediate risk of arrest more than a distant prison term.

Network lens: crime spreads through social interactions

Why do crime rates differ so much between cities that look alike on paper? Glaeser et al. (1996) build a model where each person’s choice depends on their neighbors’ choices and use it to measure social interactions from how much crime varies across places. They find variation far larger than differences in observable characteristics can explain, implying strong social interactions, which are largest for petty crimes and smallest for serious crimes such as murder. Simulation 2 is a stripped-down version of that idea.

Did you know?

Three of the ideas on this page carry famous names. Arthur Pigou proposed taxing activities that harm others in The Economics of Welfare (1920). Ronald Coase’s “The Problem of Social Cost” (1960) showed when bargaining can solve the problem instead. And Gary Becker’s “Crime and Punishment: An Economic Approach” (1968) began, he later recalled, when he was running late and weighed the cost of an illegal parking spot against the risk of a ticket.
